# Transfer Mechanism Model Tournament

This project treats transfer as a set of competing mechanistic hypotheses rather than as a single yes/no outcome. Existing longitudinal datasets such as COGITO are used to compare which learning dynamics best predict within-construct portability and between-construct changes in learnability.

The retrospective analyses are not a direct causal test of the prospective Trident Anchor–Perturb–Return intervention. Their purpose is to identify which transfer mechanisms are empirically plausible enough to justify prospective app experiments.

## Two prospective Trident hypotheses

### P1 — Plateau-triggered construct breakthrough

Train one construct on a stable backbone until the longitudinal learning curve flattens, introduce a wrapper perturbation for a bounded period, then return to the original backbone.

The key prediction is not merely recovery. The return may produce a breakthrough in the original performance frontier, expressed as one or more of:

- greater capacity;
- higher accuracy at the same capacity;
- greater resistance to lure/interference conditions;
- lower performance variability;
- faster or more stable responding;
- greater robustness under changed task surfaces.

Canonical sequence:

`BUILD → FLATTEN → PERTURB → ADAPT → RETURN → RECOVER → BREAKTHROUGH? → RESTABILISE`

### P2 — Cross-construct learnability transfer

Successful learning of construct A may alter the later rate or state-transition dynamics of learning construct B when A is an enabling or rate-limiting component for B.

The stronger claim is therefore not simply:

`change(A) correlates with change(B)`

but:

`earlier change(A) predicts later learning velocity or transition dynamics in B`

The reverse direction must always be fitted as a comparison.

## Competing models

### M0 — Task-specific fluency

Practice mainly compiles task-specific routines. Learning on task A adds little prediction of untrained-task performance once baseline ability and final trained-task performance are controlled.

This is the core null model for construct transfer.

### M1 — Surface-invariant operation

Training can strengthen an operation that is portable across task material or surface features.

Prediction: learning on a trained task predicts performance on an untrained task preserving the same operation but changing content or carrier.

COGITO examples include spatial 3-back to numerical 3-back, numerical updating to spatial updating, and alpha span to animal span.

### M2 — Construct strengthening

Transfer reflects change in a broader latent construct rather than a single trained operation.

Prediction: coordinated learning across several tasks within a construct predicts latent construct-level transfer better than any single task alone.

### M3 — Learning-dynamics transfer

How a person learns contains information about transfer beyond how high they finish.

Candidate predictors include early slope, late slope, curve shape, flattening point, asymptote, residual variability, local change-points and post-change recovery.

Prediction: trajectory features explain held-out transfer above baseline score, total gain and endpoint performance.

### M4 — Plateau/reorganisation model

Transfer is especially associated with learning trajectories that approach a local asymptote and then reorganise or renew improvement.

Prediction: plateau timing, post-plateau change-points or renewed learning predict transfer more strongly than total practice gain alone.

This is the closest retrospective analogue of the plateau-triggered perturbation proposal.

### M5 — Breadth/variation model

Portability emerges when improvement is distributed across different task forms rather than concentrated in one narrow surface.

Prediction: balanced improvement across related tasks predicts transfer better than specialist improvement on one task.

This model is especially relevant to wrapper diversity and multimodal training.

### M6 — General learnability

People differ in a relatively domain-general capacity to improve with practice.

Prediction: learning parameters across working memory, perceptual speed/evidence processing and episodic memory load on a common learnability component.

M6 is a critical competing explanation for apparent cross-construct facilitation.

### M7 — Directed facilitation

Improvement in construct A prospectively facilitates later learning in construct B.

Prediction: earlier change in A predicts later B learning velocity after controlling prior B trajectory, session/time effects and person-level learnability.

The reverse B-to-A model is compulsory.

### M8 — Reciprocal mutualist coupling

Constructs facilitate one another over time rather than influence flowing only in one direction.

Prediction: A predicts later acceleration in B and B predicts later acceleration in A, beyond shared general learnability.

This is the strongest mutualist version of cross-construct transfer.

### M9 — Bottleneck-release / threshold model

Construct A need not continuously accelerate B. Instead, B may remain constrained until A reaches a sufficient level or undergoes a meaningful transition.

Prediction: crossing an individual threshold or change-point in A is followed by increased B learning rate or a higher probability of a B state transition.

This model is particularly relevant to hierarchical Trident learning.

### M10 — Competition/interference

Concurrent learning can consume shared resources or produce interference rather than facilitation.

Prediction: periods of rapid improvement in A are followed by reduced learning velocity in B, either transiently or systematically.

This is an important falsification and alternative-mechanism model.

### M11 — State-gated transfer

Transfer depends on the learner's current latent learning state rather than current score alone.

Candidate states include SEARCH, TUNE, SATURATED, REORGANISE, RECOVER and STABLE.

Prediction: specific state transitions, particularly saturation/reorganisation and recovery, improve prediction of later transfer or cross-construct acceleration beyond conventional learning-curve measures.

## Outcome hierarchy

The tournament should distinguish several kinds of evidence:

1. **Task gain** — improvement on the practised task.
2. **Same-operation portability** — performance on an untrained same-operation/different-surface task.
3. **Construct-level transfer** — improvement in a latent construct represented by several tasks.
4. **Learning-dynamics transfer** — trajectory properties predict transfer above endpoint performance.
5. **Cross-construct learnability transfer** — change in one construct predicts later learning velocity in another.
6. **State-transition transfer** — change in one construct predicts a transition in the learning regime of another.

## Model comparison principles

Models should be compared on participant-isolated held-out prediction where practical, not selected solely by significance tests. The analysis should report predictive performance, uncertainty, parameter stability and whether effects replicate across tasks or age groups.

Important comparisons include:

- endpoint-only versus trajectory-feature models;
- continuous versus threshold facilitation;
- directed versus reciprocal coupling;
- cross-construct effects with and without a common learnability factor;
- facilitation versus interference;
- score-based versus state-based prediction.

## Bridge to the prospective Synergy IQ experiment

The retrospective tournament should be used to choose the most defensible prospective training regime. For example, if plateau/change-point dynamics and breadth predict transfer better than smooth uninterrupted practice, the app experiment should compare:

`backbone-only practice`

with

`plateau-triggered Anchor–Perturb–Return`

while matching training dose as closely as possible.

Prospective outcomes should include multidimensional performance rather than accuracy alone: capacity, accuracy, interference robustness, stability, return-to-frontier recovery, frontier extension, held-out wrapper transfer and delayed re-entry.

The longer-term question is whether a construct-transfer regime that successfully produces these changes also increases the subsequent learning rate of a different construct.
